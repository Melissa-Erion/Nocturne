To lose fat, you eat fewer calories than you burn. That part is simple. The hard part is the size of the gap: too small and nothing happens, too big and you lose muscle, energy and motivation along with the fat. Here's how to pick a deficit that works, and how to adjust it as you go.

## The short answer

Eat about **15–20% below your maintenance calories** and aim to lose about **0.5% of your body weight per week**. Anywhere from **0.25% to 1% a week** is a healthy pace for most people.

For a 70 kg (154 lb) person, 0.5% is about **0.35 kg (0.75 lb) a week**, or around 1.5 kg (3 lb) a month.

> Don't know your maintenance calories? [Work them out step by step here](/guides/how-to-calculate-macros/). It takes about five minutes.

## Why not cut harder?

A bigger deficit looks faster on paper, but it backfires in three ways:

- **Muscle goes with the fat.** Lose more than about 1% of your body weight a week and a bigger share of what you lose is muscle, which is the opposite of what you're training for.
- **Training suffers.** Low energy means weaker sessions, so your weights stall or drop.
- **You stop.** Hunger, low mood and poor sleep make a harsh diet hard to keep up. A moderate deficit is the one you're still on in week ten.

Regimen sets fat-loss calories at **18% below maintenance**, and never suggests going below your estimated BMR or 1,200 kcal (women) / 1,500 kcal (men).

## Keep your muscle while you lose fat

The deficit decides how much weight you lose. Two other things decide whether it's fat or muscle:

1. **Eat enough protein.** Aim for about 1 gram per pound of body weight (2.2 g per kg). It protects muscle and keeps you full. [Here's why that number.](/guides/how-much-protein/)
2. **Keep lifting.** Lifting tells your body the muscle is still needed. Try to keep the weights on the bar the same while you diet; holding your strength is a win.

## Judge it by the trend, not the scale

Your weight can swing 1–2 kg from one morning to the next with water, salt and food. One weigh-in tells you almost nothing. What matters is the **trend**:

1. Weigh yourself most mornings, after the bathroom and before eating.
2. Look at the **7-day average**, not single days.
3. Compare the trend over **two weeks or more** before changing anything.

## When to adjust

Every two weeks, compare your trend with the target pace:

- **Losing 0.25–1% a week?** You're on track. Change nothing.
- **Losing less than 0.25% a week** (or nothing) for two weeks? Eat about **100–200 kcal less** a day.
- **Losing more than 1% a week?** Eat about **100–200 kcal more** a day.

Take the calories out of carbs and fat, not protein. Small steps are enough; a 150 kcal change is a small portion of rice or a spoon of peanut butter.

Regimen does this check for you. Every two weeks it looks at your last four weeks of weigh-ins and, if your trend is outside that range, suggests a change of 50 to 200 kcal a day. It keeps your protein the same and takes the change from carbs (60%) and fat (40%). It's a suggestion: nothing changes until you accept it.

## Common questions

### How long should I stay in a deficit?

Most people do well with 8–16 weeks at a time, then a few weeks at maintenance before dieting again. A break helps your energy, training and motivation recover.

### Should I eat less on rest days?

You can, but you don't need to. What drives fat loss is your total over the week. Some people prefer a little less food (mostly fewer carbs) on rest days; Regimen lets you set separate rest-day targets if you want them.

### My weight went up overnight. Did the diet stop working?

No. Overnight jumps are water: a salty meal, more carbs, a hard workout, poor sleep or your cycle. Watch the 7-day average for two weeks before deciding anything.

### What about cardio?

Walking more is the easiest way to burn extra without making you hungrier. If your activity changes a lot, your maintenance calories change too, and the two-week check will catch it.

> This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a medical condition, are pregnant, or have a history of disordered eating, talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before changing how you eat.
